Post-industrial cities can undergo a \emph{functional rupture}: a discontinuity in the services, safety, and accessibility that sustain everyday life. Detroit is a canonical example, where decades of economic contraction and disinvestment created a highly heterogeneous urban fabric---revitalized pockets coexist with extensive vacancy and infrastructure decay. Detecting and localizing such ruptures matters for planning and recovery, yet the signals we typically monitor are largely static. Recent work has shown that mobility patterns reveal social structures invisible to static maps---for example, \emph{experienced income segregation} measures the income composition of places people actually visit, not just where they live \citep{moro2021mobility,nilforoshan2023mobility}. We extend this behavioral lens to urban function: route choice encodes not just \emph{where} people go, but \emph{how} they navigate---and what they avoid.

Most diagnosis of urban rupture relies on outcome indicators: vacancy rates summarize what remains, crime statistics summarize what happens, and census measures summarize who stays. These are essential, but they are \emph{consequences}. Missing from the picture is the \emph{process}: how do residents adapt their daily behavior when parts of the city stop functioning? This behavioral response is not captured in snapshots, but it is written into trajectories.

We argue that route choice is a form of behavioral sensing. Everyday trajectories encode a continual ``vote'' on the city: which corridors are preferred, which blocks are avoided, and which regions remain connected in practice. When a region is systematically bypassed, the signal is not only about geometry (roads) but also about function (what the region offers) and risk (what it costs to traverse). The challenge is that detours are often \emph{normal}: people do not minimize Euclidean distance, and even the shortest-path baseline is a poor behavioral predictor.

This paper introduces the concept of a \textbf{behavioral reference frame}. Instead of comparing observed routes to a physical baseline (shortest paths), we learn a behavioral baseline from \emph{functional} cities: a model that captures normal route-choice trade-offs under comparable origin--destination intent and time context. We then apply this learned baseline to a target city (Detroit). The mismatch between the predicted ``normal'' behavior and the realized behavior becomes a \emph{signature} of functional rupture. Mapping these residuals reveals where rupture is most behaviorally salient, and enables validation against external indicators. Prior work on avoidance detection often defines avoidance as ``departure from shortest paths'' \citep{eftelioglu2018avoidance}. But shortest paths are a poor behavioral baseline: people routinely take detours for efficiency (fast corridors), convenience (passing services), or habit even in functional cities. A behavioral reference frame absorbs these ``normal detours'' and isolates the residual that is more plausibly attributable to dysfunction.

To operationalize this idea, we build a geo-explicit representation that aligns mobility traces (WorldTrace) with multi-source urban context on a shared Detroit grid: an OpenStreetMap-derived road-proximity field as a \emph{soft} prior, SafeGraph POI semantics, and ArcGIS Wayback overhead imagery. The behavioral reference model is a route-choice generator, but it must avoid a common failure of end-to-end coordinate models: producing overly direct, near-shortest routes even when real drivers detour. We therefore use a decision--execution decomposition with an autoregressive waypoint planner so corridor-level commitments are modeled explicitly. In this paper, ``destination gravity'' is treated as a technical pitfall that prevents a model from serving as a reliable behavioral reference, rather than as the main scientific object of study.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
This paper makes three contributions to understanding how urban functional rupture manifests in human behavior.
(1) \textbf{Conceptual:} we propose \emph{behavioral reference frames} as a new lens for urban diagnosis. Unlike physical baselines (shortest paths), a behavioral reference captures the route-choice trade-offs people make in functional cities, enabling separation of ``normal detours'' from rupture-induced avoidance.
(2) \textbf{Methodological:} we develop a geo-explicit, cross-city transferable route-choice generator that can serve as a behavioral reference, and an auditable residual analysis protocol that turns prediction mismatches into spatial rupture signatures.
(3) \textbf{Empirical framework:} we construct a reproducible WorldTrace$\times$Detroit data alignment and validation protocol---combining mobility traces with soft map priors, POI semantics, and remote sensing---so that behavioral reference approaches can be evaluated against independent urban indicators in a Detroit case study.
