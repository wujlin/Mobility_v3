Urban diagnosis and mobility modeling meet at a simple premise: cities shape behavior, and behavior leaves spatial signatures. Our work sits at this intersection, drawing from (i) urban decline and functional accessibility, (ii) mobility as behavioral sensing, (iii) route choice and avoidance, and (iv) cross-city transfer with generative baselines.

\paragraph{Urban functional rupture and spatial indicators.}
Urban shrinkage and decline are typically measured through static proxies such as vacancy and vacancy-related land-use change, blight indicators, and administrative statistics. Recent work emphasizes \emph{functional} shrinkage---the mismatch between built environment supply and human activity demand---as distinct from demographic shrinkage \citep{ma2020functional}. Detroit is a canonical case where decline exhibits strong spatial heterogeneity and spillover dynamics \citep{meng2025detroit,lokhande2023spillover}. These indicators are essential, but they primarily describe outcomes rather than behavioral adaptation.

\paragraph{Mobility as a lens on urban structure.}
A growing body of work treats mobility as a signal of urban social structure. \citet{moro2021mobility} introduced \emph{experienced income segregation}, measuring segregation through the places people visit rather than where they live. \citet{nilforoshan2023mobility} showed that mobility networks reveal segregation structures invisible to residential data. This ``behavioral sensing'' paradigm motivates our approach: if mobility reveals segregation, it may also reveal functional rupture.

\paragraph{Route choice and avoidance behavior.}
Avoidance is often framed as systematic absence of movement relative to an expected baseline. \citet{eftelioglu2018avoidance} operationalized avoidance using shortest paths as the reference. However, shortest paths are a poor behavioral baseline because normal detours can be large even in functional settings. Our behavioral reference frame differs by learning the distribution of normal route choices from functional cities, so that only \emph{excess} deviation is interpreted as rupture-induced avoidance.

\paragraph{Generative models and cross-city transfer.}
Trajectory generation has advanced rapidly, including road-network constrained generative models \citep{wei2024diffrntraj} and broader surveys of unconditional and conditional generators \citep{zhu2025survey}. Cross-city generalization is increasingly studied as a technical challenge \citep{zhang2025gtg}. We invert this framing: the \emph{failure} to transfer is itself a signal. When a model trained on functional cities fails to predict Detroit's route choices under comparable intent and time context, the mismatch pattern becomes a candidate rupture signature.

\paragraph{Positioning our contribution.}
Prior work has established that mobility reveals social structure \citep{moro2021mobility,nilforoshan2023mobility}, that avoidance can be detected relative to shortest-path baselines \citep{eftelioglu2018avoidance}, and that trajectory generators can transfer across cities \citep{zhang2025gtg}. Our contribution connects these threads: we use cross-city transfer not to improve prediction accuracy, but to construct a \emph{behavioral reference frame} that separates normal route-choice trade-offs from rupture-induced avoidance---and we interpret transfer mismatch as a diagnostic signal of urban dysfunction.
