\subsection{Data-Driven Mobility Simulation for Digital Twins}
Urban mobility ``Digital Twins'' are increasingly framed as connected cyber-physical loops rather than static simulation models. In transport planning and infrastructure management, recent surveys stress clear boundary conditions that distinguish a full twin from a digital model or digital shadow, typically requiring continual data integration, a service layer, and an explicit connection between physical and digital components \citep{Nag2025ExploringDigitalTwinsTransportPlanning,Yan2023DigitalTwinTransportationInfrastructureManagement}. Within this broader ecosystem, mobility twins have been discussed as an architectural stack with data, models, and services, together with practical challenges around scalability, security, and deployment \citep{Wang2022MobilityDigitalTwin,FerreBigorra2022AdoptionUrbanDigitalTwins}. These trends align with the smart-city Digital Twin literature, where large-scale bibliometric evidence highlights rapid growth and the central role of AI, IoT, and privacy/security considerations \citep{ElAgamy2024BibliometricDigitalTwinsSmartCities}.

The development of mobility Digital Twins therefore requires high-fidelity simulation engines capable of reproducing the complex, stochastic behaviors of human mobility agents. Traditionally, mobility simulation has relied on Agent-Based Models (ABM) and microscopic kinematic rules \citep{helbing1995social}. While interpretable, these systems often require labor-intensive manual calibration of parameters and struggle to scale to city-wide heterogeneity. Consequently, data-driven approaches have emerged as a scalable alternative, learning agent behaviors directly from massive trajectory logs.

Early data-driven methods typically formulated trajectory prediction as a deterministic regression problem, utilizing recurrent architectures (RNNs, LSTMs) to model temporal dependencies \citep{alahi2016social}, or Transformer-based mechanisms to capture long-range interactions \citep{zhou2022hivt,shi2022mtr}. However, deterministic regression inherently minimizes point-wise mean error, leading to predictions that collapse towards a single conditional average. In the context of Digital Twins, this ``mode collapse'' is critical: it filters out stochastic tail events---such as rare route choices or delays---that are essential for robust risk assessment and scenario planning.

To recover this lost stochasticity, generative sequence models have been increasingly adopted. Variational Autoencoders (VAEs) and Conditional GANs have been applied to sample diverse future trajectories \citep{gupta2018socialgan}. More recently, Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs) have demonstrated superior capability in capturing complex, multimodal distributions without the training instability of GANs \citep{ho2020ddpm,zhu2023difftraj}. Innovations such as Leapfrog Diffusion \citep{mao2023led} and guidance-based control \citep{wang2024optimizingdiffusion} further enhance the controllability of these generators. Yet, a persistent challenge in applying these generative models to city-scale simulation is \emph{physical validity}: generated trajectories can exhibit ``macroscopic shrinkage'' or kinematic inconsistencies, undermining the fidelity required for engineering-grade Digital Twins.

From a system perspective, several recent efforts demonstrate how mobility simulation, real-time traffic streams, and AI modules can be combined into an operational Digital Twin workflow. Open and scalable urban mobility operating systems have been proposed to support city-scale experimentation and visualization \citep{Yeon2023DTUMOS}, and real-time synergy between traffic data streams and simulation has been emphasized for virtualizing motorway dynamics \citep{Kusic2023DigitalTwinTransportationMotorway}. Related work also highlights Digital Twin designs at corridor/freeway scales \citep{Fu2022DigitalTwinSmartFreeway} and motivates DT services through resilience and adaptability objectives in transportation systems \citep{Feng2023ResilienceTowardedDigitalTwins}. In this context, data-driven trajectory generation can be viewed as a modular AI capability that enriches the data-model layer of a mobility twin, complementing broader DT+AI integration patterns discussed in the infrastructure literature \citep{Wu2022DigitalTwinsAITransportationInfrastructure}.

\subsection{Physics-Informed Inductive Biases in Urban Computing}
To ensure that data-driven models respect the physical laws of urban dynamics, Physics-Informed Machine Learning (PIML) has become a key research frontier. In the context of mobility, ``physics'' refers to constraints imposed by road network topology, traffic flow dynamics, and kinematic limits. 
Explicit structural constraints have been integrated into diffusion models via graph-based priors \citep{shi2024gdp} or road-network-constrained generation \citep{wei2024diffrntraj}. Beyond explicit constraints, inductive biases derived from vector fields or partial differential equations (PDEs) are also employed to guide the learning process \citep{bastek2025pidm}.

Our work aligns with this philosophy but adopts a specific focus on \emph{macroscopic flow consistency}. Rather than imposing rigid kinematic equations which may limit inference speed, we employ a non-parametric navigation field---derived from historical flow statistics---as a soft physical prior. This approach anchors the generative process in the empirical reality of the city's flow dynamics, providing a balanced solution for constructing the ``behavioral layer'' of an urban Digital Twin.
