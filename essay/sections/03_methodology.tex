\subsection{Methodology: Behavioral Reference Frames via Cross-City Transfer}
Our methodology is organized around a research design goal: instantiate a \emph{behavioral reference frame} that predicts how people \emph{normally} choose routes under comparable intent and time context, then use the mismatch to detect functional rupture signatures. The generator is not a goal in itself; it exists to make the behavioral baseline well-defined and auditable.

\subsubsection{Study design: construct, apply, compare}
We instantiate the behavioral reference frame in four steps.
\textbf{(1) Learn normal route choice.} We train a route-choice generator in one or more functional reference cities, learning the distribution of corridor decisions and detours conditioned on origin--destination intent and time context.
\textbf{(2) Apply to Detroit.} We transfer the trained generator to Detroit without re-fitting to Detroit-specific behavior. This yields a ``normal-behavior'' prediction for each Detroit trip context.
\textbf{(3) Compute residuals.} We compare the predicted routes to realized Detroit routes and summarize the mismatch as residual statistics (e.g., corridor shifts, systematic detour under/over-estimation, and spatial over/under-representation).
\textbf{(4) Spatialize and validate.} We map residuals over the city and validate them by comparison with external indicators of functional rupture.

This design addresses a key ambiguity: detours can be normal efficiency trade-offs, not necessarily symptoms. By learning the baseline from functional cities, we avoid treating shortest paths as ``ground truth'' behavior.

\subsubsection{Cross-city transfer protocol (what is shared vs.\ city-specific)}
Cross-city transfer is the mechanism that turns a generator into a behavioral reference. We use the same model architecture and learning objective across cities, and align all inputs to a city grid. City-specific information enters only through geo-explicit context channels (soft road proximity, POI semantics, and overhead imagery) computed from that city's data sources. The transfer protocol is evaluated explicitly by a functional$\to$functional audit: if the reference city is truly learning normal behavior, applying it to another functional city should yield relatively small residuals compared to applying it to Detroit.

\paragraph{Reference city selection.}
Reference cities are chosen to be plausibly ``functional'' under our study definition (no well-documented large-scale rupture), to have sufficient mobility coverage, and to be comparable in scale so that route-choice trade-offs are meaningful. This selection is part of the experimental design and is not assumed to be neutral: if a reference city is atypical, the resulting baseline will be biased and the residuals will be harder to interpret.

\paragraph{Representation and conditioning.}
All inputs are represented on a shared city grid per region (Section~4). For each prediction window, we build a context tensor by stacking geo-explicit channels such as road proximity (\texttt{road\_prob}), POI semantic rasters, and (optionally) features derived from overhead imagery. We extract local patches centered at the current position (and optionally at the destination) and encode them with a lightweight CNN. Preserving spatial structure---where things are---is necessary for measuring corridor-level deviations.

\subsubsection{Planner: autoregressive waypoint decisions}
To serve as a behavioral reference, the model must reproduce normal corridor decisions rather than collapsing to overly direct routes. In preliminary experiments, simple end-to-end coordinate generators often default to near-shortest paths even when real trajectories detour. This ``over-direct'' behavior is a measurement artifact: it would make the reference frame indistinguishable from a physical shortest-path baseline. We therefore use an explicit planner that models corridor commitments.

The planner outputs a small set of anchors (wp1, wp2, end) that summarize the route at a coarse level. Anchors are generated sequentially: wp1 from history and context, then wp2 conditioned on wp1, and finally end conditioned on wp1 and wp2. Autoregression is a structural commitment: later decisions cannot ignore earlier ones, which reduces incoherent corridor switching.

In practice, each step predicts a discrete distribution over grid locations within the local patch (or over the full grid for end), conditioned on (i) the observed prefix, (ii) OD and time features, (iii) the encoded urban context, and (iv) previously chosen waypoints. At inference, this yields a distribution over plausible corridors rather than a single point estimate.

\subsubsection{Executor: deterministic trajectory realization}
Given the anchor sequence, an execution module produces a smooth trajectory. In our current design, the executor is deterministic and trained as conditional regression. This reflects a practical separation: most uncertainty comes from which corridor to take (macro), while execution given a corridor is comparatively stable and dominated by smoothness and inertia. The executor focuses on realistic motion profiles rather than on route-level diversity.

\subsubsection{Soft geographic prior (OSM) as feature and regularizer}
We avoid hard output masking. Instead, we use OSM-derived road proximity in two ways.
First, it is an input channel that provides a direct cue about likely drivable regions. Second, it appears as a soft off-road regularizer: the planner is penalized when it assigns high probability to locations far from roads. Concretely, we add a term proportional to the expected ``off-roadness'' under the predicted waypoint distributions. This design encourages feasible behavior while avoiding distribution truncation and reducing brittleness to map gaps.

\subsubsection{End-to-end training with robustness augmentation (ATR + STM)}
We train planner and executor end-to-end. We adopt two robustness augmentations:
\emph{adaptive resampling} to reduce redundancy in long segments while preserving informative motion patterns, and
\emph{trajectory masking} that hides parts of the observed prefix (random points and contiguous blocks) to encourage robustness to missing observations and prevent over-reliance on brittle local cues.

\subsubsection{Residuals as signatures (what we measure)}
Once the behavioral reference model is trained, we compute residuals between predicted normal behavior and realized Detroit behavior. We summarize residuals at multiple levels:
(i) \emph{route scalar residuals} (systematic detour under/over-estimation relative to the reference),
(ii) \emph{corridor residuals} (shifts between parallel corridors or destination-side approach lanes), and
(iii) \emph{spatial residual maps} (cells that are over- or under-represented relative to the reference).
These residuals are the primary scientific outputs; they are then compared to external rupture indicators.

In plain terms, the residual asks: \emph{given the same origin, destination, and time context, what parts of the city does the reference model expect travelers to traverse, and which parts do they actually traverse?} Cells that are consistently predicted but rarely visited form an ``avoidance field'', while cells that are unexpectedly over-visited suggest compensatory routing or functional substitution.

\paragraph{Behavioral vs.\ physical reference.}
We report all residual analyses relative to the behavioral reference and contrast them with a physical reference frame (shortest-path baseline). This comparison is essential because a city can contain normal detours even without rupture; the behavioral reference is designed to absorb these normal trade-offs so that only excess deviation remains.

\subsubsection{Validity and diffusion gating (to protect the reference)}
Because residuals are interpreted as evidence, we adopt a validity-first protocol: we do not interpret residuals until basic feasibility is satisfied under an audit mask. Diffusion is introduced only after a valid single-mode baseline is established, and is used to represent multi-modal \emph{normal} route choice rather than to mask feasibility gaps.
