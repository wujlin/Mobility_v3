This section reports results once the cross-city behavioral reference is instantiated (reference cities selected and transfer audited). Results are organized around the hypotheses in Section~3 and the \emph{spatially specific} rupture-signature outputs. We focus on structure-level evidence---maps and corridor substitutions---rather than on point-wise prediction error alone.

We refer to the spatialized residual map as a \emph{behavioral avoidance field}: locations and corridors that the behavioral reference predicts should be traversed under normal trade-offs but are systematically under-realized in Detroit. Complementarily, we report \emph{substitution structure}: where the displaced mobility mass concentrates, revealing which corridors compensate for avoided regions.

\subsection{Global detour scalars do not distinguish rupture}
A natural intuition is that rupture-induced avoidance should increase average detours: if travelers bypass dysfunctional areas, routes should become longer and deviate more from straight lines. Our data do not support this naive expectation. Using two scalar summaries---the length ratio (route length divided by OD straight-line distance) and maximum deviation ratio (maximum lateral deviation from the OD chord, normalized by OD distance)---Detroit and Columbus are nearly indistinguishable in median length ratio ($1.297$ vs.\ $1.296$), and Detroit is \emph{more direct} in median maximum deviation ratio ($0.166$ vs.\ $0.197$). This null result is informative: functional rupture does not simply make people ``drive longer.''

The implication is that rupture signatures, if they exist, must lie in \emph{spatial organization} rather than global detour magnitude: which corridors are under-used relative to normal expectations, which alternative corridors absorb the displaced flow, and where sharp behavioral boundaries emerge. This motivates the behavioral avoidance field as the primary object of analysis.

\subsection{H1: Functional$\to$Functional Transfer Audit}
We will evaluate transfer between two functional cities to establish that the learned baseline represents normal route choice rather than city-specific idiosyncrasies. The key output is a low residual baseline for functional$\to$functional transfer.

\subsection{H2: Detroit Rupture Signatures}
We will first report a scalar baseline (e.g., aggregate detour differences and tract-level correlations) to show that a signal exists but is weak when spatial structure is collapsed. We then construct and visualize the behavioral avoidance field over Detroit, and characterize its morphology: whether residuals form contiguous fracture zones, sharp behavioral boundaries, or diffuse gradients. We will quantify corridor substitution by comparing predicted and realized corridor usage for matched OD contexts, and identify which corridors are systematically under-used and which alternatives absorb the flow.

\subsection{H3: Behavioral vs.\ Physical Reference}
We use a \emph{physical reference frame}---shortest paths on the road network---as a negative control. Concretely, for each realized segment we compute a soft-constraint shortest path on OpenStreetMap using a road-proximity prior (higher cost farther from roads), then rasterize the expected and observed routes to probability maps on the same grid. We summarize their mismatch within the expected support region (cells with non-negligible expected mass).

This test reveals a fundamental limitation of physical baselines. Even in Columbus (a functional reference city), shortest paths are an overly diffuse expectation: the shortest-path support covers $\sim$17\% of grid cells, but only $\sim$21\% of these cells are ever traversed (support-overlap ratio $0.209$). Conversely, only $\sim$30\% of the shortest-path probability mass lies on cells that are actually used ($0.296$). In other words, shortest paths predict many ``possible'' cells that humans simply do not take, because real behavior concentrates on a small number of corridors.

Detroit exhibits the same pattern, and the mismatch is more severe: within the expected support, the overlap ratio drops to $0.156$, and the expected mass that lands on observed cells drops to $0.245$. Critically, the existence of this large mismatch in a functional city shows that a shortest-path baseline would generate massive false-positive ``avoidance'' (cells that are unused, not avoided). This motivates our behavioral reference frame: the baseline must encode the \emph{normal corridor concentration} of route choice before residuals can be interpreted as rupture signatures.

\subsection{External Validation and Ablations}
We will validate avoidance-field structure against independent indicators (e.g., vacancy, income, population change, and optional safety proxies) using tract-level aggregation and spatial statistics. We will also conduct ablations on context sources (soft map prior, POI semantics, overhead imagery) and model structure (autoregressive planning) to assess which inputs are necessary for spatial specificity. Diffusion will be evaluated only after a stable single-mode reference is established, to isolate its role in multi-modal \emph{normal} route choice rather than feasibility repair.
