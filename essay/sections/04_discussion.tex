\paragraph{Multi-scale tension: individual choice vs.\ collective flow.}
Our findings highlight a fundamental methodological tension in urban mobility modeling: the conflict between \emph{microscopic fidelity} and \emph{macroscopic consistency}. In traditional deterministic regression, the model acts as a ``cautious planner'', collapsing the diverse potential of an urban grid into a single, mean-reverted path. While this minimizes point-wise error, it erases the spatial entropy---the branching of possibilities at intersections---that defines a living city. Denoising diffusion models successfully recover this entropy, as evidenced by their superior best-of-$K$ coverage. However, they introduce a new risk: the ``drift toward the mean'' during iterative denoising, which manifests as macroscopic shrinkage.

\paragraph{Shrinkage as a geographic reflection of spatial entropy.}
The systematic under-estimation of displacement (low MSD/Rog) in Phase B v1.0 is more than a technical calibration error; it reflects a model's struggle to maintain \emph{directional persistence} across high-entropy urban junctions. At each denoising step, if the local navigation field or condition is ambiguous (e.g., a complex intersection), the generator tends to favor a conservative, low-velocity correction. Over multiple time steps, these cautious micro-adjustments accumulate into a macroscopic loss of momentum. Our analysis shows that post-hoc scaling is insufficient because it simply amplifies the model's directional uncertainty.

\paragraph{A hierarchical multi-scale framework via residual diffusion.}
In Phase B v1.1, we demonstrate that a structural remedy---residual decomposition---outperforms naive loss regularization. By freezing a deterministic baseline as a low-frequency prior, we ``anchor'' the global scale of the trajectory. The diffusion process then focuses on stochastic residuals, i.e., plausible deviations from the mean flow. This creates a \textbf{hierarchical multi-scale framework}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Macroscopic Anchor (Baseline)}: Captures the fundamental OD-driven displacement and speed profile.
  \item \textbf{Mesoscopic Guide (Nav Field)}: Injects historical flow patterns and directional constraints.
  \item \textbf{Microscopic Variability (Diffusion Residual)}: Learns the multi-modal distribution of human choice and local route variations.
\end{itemize}
This decomposition ensures that the generated futures are both diverse and physically grounded, bridging the gap between probabilistic coverage and macroscopic realism.

\paragraph{Implications for urban analytics and risk-aware planning.}
The value of this framework extends beyond academic metrics. For urban planners, the ability to generate \emph{calibrated} multimodal futures enables robust scenario testing. For instance, in emergency evacuation modeling or congestion management, knowing the ``worst-case'' dispersion or the probability of multiple route clusters can be more actionable than a single average prediction. Our geographic visualizations suggest that these models can reveal latent ``flow corridors'' and ``spatial divergence zones'' that are largely invisible to deterministic baselines.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.}
Despite the success of the residual approach, our current implementation remains grid-based, which limits awareness of fine-grained road network topology. Future work should explore (i) road-graph-aware diffusion to better align with physical infrastructure, and (ii) dynamic coupling between the navigation field and real-time traffic signals. Furthermore, the sensitivity of macro-level statistics to the choice of the deterministic prior suggests that the ``quality of the anchor'' is a critical bottleneck. Exploring better-calibrated low-frequency priors---possibly physics-based kinematic models---remains a promising frontier for city-scale trajectory generation.
