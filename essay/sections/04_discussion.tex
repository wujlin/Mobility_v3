\subsection{The Multi-Scale Tension: Individual Choice vs. Collective Flow}
Our findings highlight a fundamental methodological tension in urban mobility modeling: the conflict between \emph{microscopic fidelity} and \emph{macroscopic consistency}. In traditional deterministic regression, the model acts as a "cautious planner," collapsing the diverse potential of an urban grid into a single, mean-reverted path. While this minimizes point-wise error, it erases the spatial entropy—the branching of possibilities at intersections—that defines a living city. Denoising diffusion models successfully recover this entropy, as evidenced by their superior best-of-$K$ coverage. However, they introduce a new risk: the ``drift toward the mean'' during iterative denoising, which manifests as macroscopic shrinkage.

\subsection{Shrinkage as a Geographic Reflection of Spatial Entropy}
The systematic under-estimation of displacement (low MSD/Rog) in Phase B v1.0 is more than a technical calibration error; it reflects a model's struggle to maintain \emph{directional persistence} across high-entropy urban junctions. At each denoising step, if the local navigation field or condition is ambiguous (e.g., a complex intersection), the generator tends to favor a conservative, low-velocity correction. Over multiple time steps, these cautious micro-adjustments accumulate into a macroscopic loss of momentum. Our analysis shows that post-hoc scaling is insufficient because it simply amplifies the model's directional uncertainty.

\subsection{Towards a Hierarchical Multi-Scale Framework: Residual Diffusion}
In Phase B v1.1, we demonstrate that a structural remedy—residual decomposition—outperforms naive loss regularization. By freezing a deterministic baseline as a low-frequency prior, we essentially "anchor" the global scale of the trajectory. The diffusion processes then focus solely on the stochastic residuals, i.e., the plausible deviations from the mean flow. This creates a \textbf{Hierarchical Multi-Scale Framework}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Macroscopic Anchor (Baseline)}: Captures the fundamental OD-driven displacement and speed profile.
  \item \textbf{Mesoscopic Guide (Nav Field)}: Injects historical flow patterns and directional constraints.
  \item \textbf{Microscopic Variability (Diffusion Residual)}: Learns the multi-modal distribution of human choice and local route variations.
\end{itemize}
This decomposition ensures that the generated futures are both diverse and physically grounded, bridging the gap between probabilistic coverage and macroscopic realism.

\subsection{Implications for Urban Analytics and Risk-Aware Planning}
The value of this framework extends beyond academic metrics. For urban planners, the ability to generate \emph{calibrated} multi-modal futures enables robust scenario testing. For instance, in emergency evacuation modeling or congestion management, knowing the ``worst-case'' dispersion or the probability of multiple route clusters is more critical than a single average prediction. Our geographic visualizations demonstrate that these models can reveal hidden "flow corridors" and "spatial divergence zones" that are invisible to deterministic baselines.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
Despite the success of the residual approach, our current implementation remains grid-based, which limits the model's awareness of fine-grained road network topology. Future work should explore (i) road-graph-aware diffusion to better align with physical infrastructure, and (ii) dynamic coupling between the navigation field and real-time traffic signals. Furthermore, the sensitivity of macro-level stats to the choice of the deterministic prior suggests that the "quality of the anchor" is a critical bottleneck. Exploring better-calibrated low-frequency priors—perhaps physics-based kinematic models—remains a promising frontier for city-scale trajectory generation.
