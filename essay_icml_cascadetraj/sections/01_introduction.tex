\label{sec:intro}

Route generation is a core primitive for mobility simulation, transport planning, and urban computing. However, real-world routes are neither purely geometric curves nor deterministic shortest paths: they are shaped by \emph{road topology} (connectivity, bottlenecks, and hierarchy) and by \emph{urban semantics} (activity centers, functional land use, and time-dependent demand).

This creates a tension in existing trajectory generators. \emph{Map-free} models that generate continuous coordinates directly often produce trajectories that drift off-road or collapse to trivial straight lines, because the road network is a measure-zero set in continuous space and soft regularizers provide only weak guidance. At the other extreme, \emph{map-aware} baselines that rely on shortest paths or hand-crafted costs can be structurally valid but behaviorally implausible: they do not capture multi-modal corridor choice and do not express how semantics (e.g., POIs, road hierarchy, and time-of-day) shift route preferences.

\paragraph{Redefining realism.}
We distinguish two notions of realism:
\textbf{structural realism} (geometric validity, e.g., on-road trajectories) and
\textbf{behavioral realism} (contextual plausibility, i.e., humans choose different corridors under different temporal--spatial conditions).
Structural realism is largely an engineering constraint; behavioral realism is the scientific question.
Crucially, behavioral realism cannot be achieved by learning only $p(\text{route}\mid o,d)$: diversity without context becomes indiscriminate randomness rather than meaningful behavioral differences.

\paragraph{Why naïve fixes are insufficient.}
One can enforce roads by post-hoc snapping or map matching, but this changes the sample distribution and can destroy the very behavioral signals we aim to model. Likewise, graph routing with random perturbations can create diverse paths, but the diversity is not tied to temporal--spatial context and therefore does not explain \emph{why} travelers prefer one corridor over another.

\paragraph{Key insight.}
Trip-scale route choice is naturally hierarchical. At a coarse level, travelers choose among a small number of plausible \emph{corridors} connecting an origin--destination (OD) pair; at a fine level, they execute a realistic continuous trajectory within the chosen corridor. Both levels are modulated by semantics and temporal context. This suggests separating \emph{discrete corridor selection} from \emph{continuous trajectory execution}, and letting road topology provide a structural prior only where it is essential.

\paragraph{CascadeTraj.}
We propose \textbf{CascadeTraj}, a hierarchical route generation framework that combines road network topology for structural realism with urban semantic features for behavioral fidelity. The decision stage selects corridors on the road graph conditioned on semantic context; the execution stage generates realistic trajectories within the selected corridor. This design turns the measure-zero road constraint into a first-class discrete decision, while preserving flexibility for continuous dynamics.

We identify a fundamental limitation: map-free continuous generation cannot reliably converge to road corridors at trip scale, even with soft road priors. This motivates a core architectural insight---treating corridor choice as a \emph{discrete decision} on the road graph rather than a continuous optimization toward a measure-zero target. Crucially, temporal--spatial semantics enter this discrete decision as first-class conditioning signals, enabling context-dependent behavioral diversity rather than random perturbations. We substantiate the diagnostic with waypoint audits and case-level visualizations (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), and present CascadeTraj as a mechanism that unifies structural realism with behaviorally grounded, context-conditioned route choice.

Sections~\ref{sec:experiments}--\ref{sec:results} establish the diagnostic evidence: waypoint audits and case visualizations demonstrate the measure-zero failure mode of map-free generation, motivating the discrete corridor formulation.
