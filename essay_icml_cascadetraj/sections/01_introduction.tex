% Introduction - ICML style: problem-driven narrative

Generative models for human mobility are increasingly important for transportation simulation, urban digital twins, and location-based services.
While short-horizon trajectory prediction has matured rapidly, \emph{route generation}---sampling an entire trip-level path from origin to destination---remains underexplored as a structured generation problem.

The core challenge is \emph{corridor-level multi-modality}.
For a fixed origin--destination (OD) pair, real-world travelers choose among topologically distinct alternatives: a highway bypass, a scenic route through residential streets, or a shortcut via service roads.
These corridors are far apart in coordinate space yet intent-equivalent---they all reach the same destination.
This multi-modality is fundamentally \emph{discrete}: the primary uncertainty lies in \emph{which corridor} to take, while within-corridor variation is comparatively smooth.

End-to-end coordinate generators---including recent diffusion and flow-matching models---struggle under this structure.
When trained to reconstruct diverse corridors, they tend toward destructive averaging (blending distinct paths into an invalid ``middle'' trajectory) or long-horizon drift (accumulating errors over hundreds of steps).
Candidate-set formulations (e.g., classify among $K$-shortest paths) sidestep generation but can miss ground-truth corridors entirely: our diagnostics show that heuristic candidates achieve only $\approx 0.21$ median edge-Jaccard match to ground truth.

We propose \textbf{CascadeTraj}, a hierarchical decision--execution framework that makes corridor choice explicit.
The key insight is to decompose route generation into two stages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Decision stage}: A short-horizon autoregressive model samples a \emph{sparse waypoint commitment}---3--5 intermediate graph nodes that define the corridor---conditioned on time, road hierarchy, and OD.
    \item \textbf{Execution stage}: A* search connects waypoints into a complete, graph-valid path, guaranteeing topological feasibility by construction.
\end{enumerate}
By reducing the autoregressive horizon from hundreds of nodes to a handful of waypoints, CascadeTraj avoids compounding error while preserving the expressiveness to capture corridor-level diversity.

We evaluate CascadeTraj on WorldTrace trip segments from Detroit and Columbus.
Our experiments establish two diagnostic gates before model comparison:
(i) \emph{candidate-set coverage}---showing that $K$-shortest heuristics can have low corridor coverage, motivating learned commitments; and
(ii) \emph{semantic informativeness}---showing that time-of-day and road-tier features carry measurable signal for corridor variability (AUC $\approx 0.80$ on multi-modal OD groups).
Under these gates, CascadeTraj achieves near-perfect route completion and improves corridor match over node-level autoregressive baselines.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item We identify corridor-level multi-modality as a structural challenge for trip-level route generation and formalize diagnostic gates for candidate coverage and semantic informativeness.
    \item We propose CascadeTraj, a decision--execution cascade that samples sparse waypoint commitments and executes them via graph search, achieving high completion rates with improved corridor fidelity.
    \item We provide empirical evidence that time and road-hierarchy context carries predictive signal for corridor choice, motivating context-conditioned generation beyond shortest-path heuristics.
\end{itemize}
