\paragraph{Short-horizon trajectory prediction vs.\ route generation.}
Most trajectory prediction work targets short horizons: given a brief observation window, predict near-future displacements.
Modern architectures (e.g., Transformers \citep{vaswani2017attention}) and generative variants have pushed accuracy forward, but these settings typically do not require committing to a full-trip route under origin--destination intent.
In contrast, full-trip route generation must represent corridor-level alternatives and ensure feasibility on a transportation network.

\paragraph{End-to-end continuous generators.}
A natural extension is to train an end-to-end generator that outputs an entire coordinate sequence, using latent-variable models \citep{kingma2014auto} or diffusion models \citep{ho2020ddpm}.
However, corridor-level multi-modality is topological and can be geometrically far apart, making continuous output spaces prone to destructive averaging or long-horizon drift.
We treat such models as diagnostic baselines rather than as a foundation for corridor modeling.

\paragraph{Candidate-set routing and path ranking.}
Classical route choice and path-ranking pipelines often enumerate a finite candidate set (e.g., $K$-shortest paths) and then select or weight candidates.
This strategy is attractive because it guarantees feasibility, but it implicitly assumes that the candidate set covers the relevant corridors.
Our candidate-coverage gate shows that this assumption can fail in practice even when GT trajectories are well aligned to the road graph, motivating decision mechanisms that do not depend on a fixed candidate set.

\paragraph{Learning to plan on graphs.}
Graph-based decision models (e.g., autoregressive next-hop prediction or pointer-style decoding over nodes) avoid infeasible continuous averages by constraining outputs to graph neighbors.
Their main challenge is long-horizon compounding error when generating long paths.
Our method addresses this by moving the decision horizon from node-level sequences (hundreds of steps) to waypoint commitments (3--5 steps), while delegating feasibility and connectivity to A* execution.

\paragraph{Hierarchical planning with waypoints/subgoals.}
Hierarchical formulations separate coarse decisions from fine execution by introducing subgoals or waypoints.
We instantiate this idea on road graphs: the decision stage samples sparse waypoint commitments conditioned on time and road hierarchy, and the executor deterministically converts commitments into valid graph paths.
Optional continuous refinement (diffusion/flow) can be layered on top to model within-corridor variability.
