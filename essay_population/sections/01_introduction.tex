\paragraph{From short-horizon forecasting to route generation.}
Urban mobility traces record how people realize intent through space: not only \emph{where} they go, but \emph{how} they get there.
Recent progress in sequence models and diffusion models \citep{vaswani2017attention,ho2020ddpm} has made short-horizon trajectory prediction increasingly accurate.
Yet many practical and scientific tasks require a different capability: \emph{route generation} over a full trip.
Given an origin--destination intent and context (e.g., departure time and neighborhood semantics), a route generator must sample an entire path that is realistic, feasible, and diverse.

\paragraph{Why long-horizon route generation fails.}
We observe two recurrent failure modes when extending generative modeling from seconds to full trips.
First, intent can drift: purely autoregressive rollouts accumulate small errors and become globally inconsistent with the destination.
Second, feasibility can break: when emphasizing diversity (e.g., via latent-variable models \citep{kingma2014auto} or standard diffusion), generators often hallucinate physically invalid paths that cut through obstacles or deviate systematically from the road network---especially when explicit map supervision is absent or unreliable.

\paragraph{Root cause: topological multi-modality and destructive averaging.}
The core difficulty is \emph{topological multi-modality}.
Even for the same origin and destination, multiple corridor-level choices can be equally reasonable.
End-to-end coordinate generators trained with likelihood or Euclidean losses therefore face a destructive ``averaging'' pressure: the mean of several feasible routes is often an infeasible straight line.
More importantly, this multi-modality is \emph{structured}: corridor-level alternatives are discrete and topological, while variations \emph{within} a corridor are comparatively continuous.
Representing this structure only implicitly in a continuous coordinate output space leads to brittle long-horizon behavior: samples can collapse to destructive averages or drift off-road and become destination-inconsistent.
This suggests that route generation should be modeled as a \emph{decision--execution} process rather than a single homogeneous sequence problem, with an explicit \emph{corridor commitment} variable that separates discrete corridor choice from continuous geometric realization.

\paragraph{A diagnostic baseline: averaging vs.\ blur in continuous output space.}
We first provide diagnostic evidence that corridor-level multi-modality is not well represented in a continuous coordinate output space.
On segment-level routes, deterministic regression collapses to overly direct ``average'' routes, while stochastic end-to-end generators can drift and become destination-inconsistent.
These failure modes motivate a representation shift: corridor choice should be an explicit \emph{structured} decision rather than an implicit continuous optimization.

Beyond model behavior, we also diagnose the \emph{candidate-set} approach.
We find that heuristic candidate sets such as $K$-shortest paths can have low coverage of ground-truth corridors, even when ground-truth trajectories are well aligned to the road network.
This makes ``classify among $K$ candidates'' an unreliable mechanism for learning corridor distributions and motivates learning corridor commitments directly on the road graph.

\paragraph{CascadeTraj: hierarchical route generation via corridor commitment.}
We propose \textbf{CascadeTraj}, a hierarchical generative framework for route generation.
At the \emph{decision} stage, the model samples a sparse waypoint sequence on a road graph as an explicit corridor commitment.
At the \emph{execution} stage, the model converts this commitment into a valid corridor path by connecting adjacent waypoints with A* (and optionally refines geometry with a continuous executor).
This decomposition turns a brittle one-shot generation problem into two conditional problems with clearer responsibilities: (i) committing to a coherent topological plan, and (ii) executing a physically plausible realization.

\paragraph{Soft priors and optional macro statistics.}
To encourage feasibility without collapsing support, we incorporate map information as \emph{soft} priors and treat the road graph as a structural constraint at the execution stage (A*).
We use a minimal context set in the decision stage (time and road hierarchy) to test whether corridor commitments can be meaningfully context-conditioned.
The paper's focus remains the generative architecture for the \emph{route itself}, not population synthesis.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Finding 1 (Diagnosis):} we characterize structural failures of end-to-end coordinate generators under corridor-level multi-modality (destructive averaging and long-horizon drift).
  \item \textbf{Finding 2 (Mechanism):} we show that heuristic candidate sets (e.g., $K$-shortest paths) can have low corridor coverage, motivating explicit corridor commitments learned on the road graph rather than classification among predefined candidates.
  \item \textbf{Method:} we propose a graph-based decision--execution route generator where a short-horizon waypoint AR model samples corridor commitments conditioned on time and road hierarchy, and A* executes these commitments into valid graph paths.
\end{itemize}
