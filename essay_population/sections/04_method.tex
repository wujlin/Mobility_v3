\subsection{Overview}
We model route generation as a decision--execution hierarchy with a latent skeleton $z$ (e.g., waypoints).
The conditional distribution factorizes as:
\begin{equation}
  p_\theta(\tau \mid c) = \sum_{z} p_\theta(z \mid c)\, p_\theta(\tau \mid z, c).
\end{equation}
In practice, we sample $z$ and then generate $\tau$ conditioned on $z$.

\subsection{Decision: waypoint skeleton}
We represent the route skeleton as an ordered waypoint sequence $z = (w_1,\dots,w_K)$.
A lightweight generative planner models $p_\theta(z \mid c)$ (e.g., autoregressive or diffusion), and must support multi-modal sampling to represent corridor-level alternatives.

\paragraph{Optional covariates (ablation).}
The framework admits additional inference-observable covariates (e.g., POI/land-use rasters, imagery-derived context, or census attributes) as extra conditioning channels.
Empirically, we find that richer semantic representations provide limited and inconsistent benefit for corridor commitment in our setting, and therefore treat them strictly as ablations rather than a core mechanism.

\subsection{Execution: continuous route generation}
Conditioned on $z$, the execution module generates a full-resolution route, modeling local geometry and motion.
We leave the specific choice (diffusion / flow matching) as an implementation detail and focus on the hierarchy and conditioning interfaces.
In practice, we treat execution as a refinement around the skeleton and scale the residual component to preserve corridor commitments when composing decision and execution.

\paragraph{Commitment-preserving refinement.}
Let $\tau_{\text{skel}}$ denote the polyline induced by the sampled skeleton $z$ (e.g., piecewise-linear interpolation),
and let the execution module predict a residual velocity sequence $\Delta v_{1:T}$ conditioned on $(z,c)$.
We form the final route by applying a scaled residual update, with a single scalar $\alpha \in [0,1]$:
\begin{equation}
  v_t = v^{\text{skel}}_t + \alpha \,\Delta v_t,\quad t=1,\dots,T.
\end{equation}
When $\alpha$ is too large, the executor can overwrite corridor commitments; choosing a small $\alpha$ yields a weak refinement that preserves corridor coverage while improving local geometric fidelity.

\paragraph{Endpoint anchoring (sampling-time).}
\label{sec:endpoint-anchoring}
At sampling time, we apply a lightweight endpoint anchoring step that enforces intent consistency by making the generated route end exactly at the given destination.
Concretely, after sampling a velocity sequence, we compute the residual displacement error relative to $(o,d)$ and distribute it uniformly across steps.
This isolates corridor commitment and shape modeling from trivial endpoint drift and makes endpoint-only metrics less informative for comparing models.

\subsection{Soft map priors for feasibility}
We incorporate map-derived signals (e.g., road proximity) as soft priors in training and/or sampling, avoiding hard masking of the output space.
This design prevents the generator from collapsing its support to an externally defined feasible set while still discouraging physically implausible routes.
In our experiments, we operationalize this as an execution-stage refinement driven by OSM-derived road probability / distance-to-road fields (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
Consistent with our stage-specific design principle, we do not rely on these map priors for corridor commitment.
